What is a company worth, and what does the stock market already believe?

A discounted cash flow model answers the first question. This one also answers the second, by running the same math backwards. Move the sliders below and watch both answers change.

How it works, in four steps

1

Forecast the cash

Start from today's revenue. Grow it each year, apply the operating margin, subtract tax, equipment spending and working capital needs. What is left is free cash flow, the money the business could hand to its owners. 		2

Shrink future dollars

A dollar ten years out is worth less than a dollar today, and a risky dollar is worth less than a safe one. Each year's cash is divided down by the required return (WACC) to get its value today. 		3

Add up and divide

Sum the ten years plus a terminal value for everything after year 10, subtract debt, add cash, divide by the share count. That is the fair value per share, your view. 		4

Run it in reverse

Instead of guessing growth and getting a price, take the real price and search for the growth that produces it. That is what the market is betting on. Compare it with history and you have an argument, not just a number.

Forward and reverse are not two models. They are one function solved for different unknowns, which is why the notebook can check itself: value a company forward, feed that price into the reverse solver, and it must recover the growth rate it started with.

Words used here

TermPlain English
Free cash flowCash the business generates after paying for what it needs to keep running and growing. The thing being valued.
DiscountingTranslating a future amount into what it is worth today. More time or more risk means a bigger haircut.
WACCWeighted average cost of capital. The blended return the company's lenders and shareholders require. Used as the discount rate.
Operating marginOperating profit as a share of revenue, before interest and tax.
Terminal valueA single number standing in for all cash flows after the ten year forecast, assuming steady modest growth forever.
Implied growthThe growth rate that makes the model's price equal the market's price. Read it as "what the market must believe".
CAGRCompound annual growth rate. The single yearly rate that gets you from the start value to the end value over several years.
BetaHow much a stock moves relative to the whole market. Feeds into the required return. The notebook estimates it from five years of weekly prices.
